Risks of Poorly Managed Conventional Agriculture

Understanding environmental, economic, and agronomic vulnerabilities arising from improper chemical management.

A Balanced, Objective Agronomic Perspective

Conventional agriculture has historically played a critical role in global food security and grain self-sufficiency. Modern agrochemicals are not inherently or automatically catastrophic under strict scientific discipline. However, in many rural smallholder tracts, misapplication, over-dosage, uncalibrated spraying schedules, and lack of protective gear create severe long-term environmental degradation and financial vulnerabilities. Risk severity is fundamentally determined by the active ingredient, dosage, timing, soil texture, crop stage, and application stewardship.

Soil Acidification & Biological Depletion
Soil Health Threat

Continuous, unilateral application of high-analysis synthetic nitrogenous fertilisers (such as excessive urea) without replenishing bulky organic manures results in:

  • Accelerated oxidation of native soil organic carbon (SOC).
  • Soil compaction, pan formation, and reduced porosity.
  • Drastic reduction in earthworm density and beneficial rhizospheric bacteria.
  • Soil acidification or secondary salinisation in poorly drained soils.
Groundwater Leaching & Eutrophication
Hydrological Threat

Plants absorb only an estimated 30% to 50% of synthetic nitrogen applied. The unabsorbed surplus fraction leaches into subsoil aquifers as nitrates:

  • Elevated nitrate concentrations in village borewells posing infant health hazards (Methemoglobinemia).
  • Nutrient runoff into village tanks and reservoirs triggering explosive algal blooms and aquatic hypoxia.
Pesticide Resistance & Pest Resurgence
Agronomic Threat

Repeated applications of identical chemical classes (e.g., synthetic pyrethroids or organophosphates) exert heavy selection pressure:

  • Target pests evolve physiological resistance, demanding escalating dosages and costlier molecules.
  • Indiscriminate toxicity wipes out beneficial predators (spiders, parasitoids), precipitating secondary pest outbreaks (e.g., brown planthopper outbreaks in paddy).
Occupational Health & Debt Vulnerability
Socio-Economic Threat

Field spraying without Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) exposes rural operators to acute and chronic neurological, respiratory, and dermatological conditions. Economically, when farmers purchase escalating quantities of expensive branded inputs on informal high-interest credit, adverse weather or market price volatility triggers devastating debt stress.

How Proper Management Mitigates These Risks

For growers who still utilize conventional products during transition phases, adherence to Good Agricultural Practices (GAP) is vital:

1. Soil Test-Based Application: Never apply nitrogen or phosphorus blindly. Test soil electrical conductivity, pH, and organic carbon every two seasons.
2. Strict Economic Threshold Levels (ETL): Spray pesticides only when pest populations surpass verified damage thresholds, not on calendar schedules.
3. Integrated Nutrient Management (INM): Combine minimum chemical fertiliser with 70% organic manures and biofertilisers to preserve soil microbes.
